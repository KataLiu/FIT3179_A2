Observation footprint

Where do our records reach?

Citizen-science observations build a shared picture of Australia, but record totals describe reporting activity rather than bird abundance.

Key finding

From 2018 to 2024, annual eBird records grew by 136%, while Birdata records grew by 32%. This widening gap reflects platform reporting activity and coverage, not a national increase in bird abundance.

03

Time and place

How do reports change through the year?

Seasonal peaks, platform shares and changing state rankings reveal when and where the national record is being assembled.

Key finding

October is the clearest reporting peak in both platforms in 2024, contributing 12.3% of eBird records and 11.8% of Birdata records. Geography matters too: Birdata supplies 26.1% of South Australia's combined records, the highest state share that year.

Where does BirdLife Australia's Birdata contribute more records?

Birdata is BirdLife Australia's national platform for submitting bird observations. Colour shows Birdata's share of combined Birdata and eBird records in each state or territory. The percentage reflects reporting activity, not bird abundance.

When are bird observations reported?

Monthly share of each platform's annual records reveals seasonal reporting patterns. A higher percentage means that more of that year's observations were submitted in that month.

Which states lead Australia's bird-reporting activity?

Leadership shifted between Queensland and Victoria, while New South Wales rose to second by 2024. Rank is based on combined eBird and Birdata records, not bird abundance.

Community contribution

The people behind the picture

Thousands of participants turn local observations into checklists, species records and a growing evidence base for wildlife research.

Key finding

In 2025, 63,990 people submitted 153,855 checklists and counted more than five million birds across 621 species. Community participation turns many local observations into a nationwide research resource.

How large is Australia's annual bird-count community?

Each square represents approximately 2,000 participants; labels retain the reported totals. Only the three years confirmed in BirdLife Australia primary sources are shown.

What did the community contribute in 2025?

The 2025 Aussie Bird Count turned thousands of individual observations into a nationwide wildlife dataset. Australian Magpie was the most counted species nationally.

!

What the records can tell us

Citizen-science records help identify spatial patterns, seasonal activity and knowledge gaps. More records do not automatically mean more birds: observer effort, platform use and accessibility affect what appears in the data.

About the data

Core observation comparisons cover 2018–2024, the common verified period used by the processed state, month and species-location files. The 2025 community figures come from BirdLife Australia's confirmed Aussie Bird Count results. Records measure reporting activity rather than bird abundance.

National, monthly and state totals are server-computed counts. Species locations and taxonomic composition use matched stratified samples; sample percentages are not population estimates. Data accessed 2 October 2026.
